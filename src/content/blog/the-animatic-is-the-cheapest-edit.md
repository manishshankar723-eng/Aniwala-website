"Can we skip the animatic and go straight to animation? We are tight on time."

We hear this on roughly one project in three, and we understand the instinct
completely. The animatic looks like an intermediate deliverable — a rough version
of a thing you will get properly later. Cutting it feels like removing a step
rather than removing work.

It is the opposite. The animatic is the only stage where the *structure* of a
piece is still negotiable, and structure is what almost every failed project got
wrong.

## What an animatic actually tests

An animatic is the boards cut to time against a scratch track. It is ugly. Panels
hold too long, the drawings are rough, the voiceover is somebody in the studio
reading off a phone.

And it tells you, definitively, four things:

- **Does it fit?** A ninety-second script is not a ninety-second film. Read aloud
  at a comfortable pace with room to breathe, most scripts run fifteen to twenty
  per cent long. You find this out in the animatic or you find it out in the grade.
- **Does the order work?** Whether the reveal lands before or after the
  explanation changes the whole piece. Moving a panel takes seconds. Moving a
  finished animated section takes a week.
- **Is anything missing?** The shot you need to make the transition make sense,
  which nobody noticed was absent because everyone had the whole idea in their
  head already.
- **Is anything redundant?** Usually two shots doing the same job. Cutting one at
  animatic stage saves its entire production cost.

None of these are questions animation answers better. Animation answers *"is this
beautiful"*. Structure has to be settled first, or you get a beautiful film that
does not work.

## The maths, roughly

Take a sixty-second explainer with twelve scenes.

Fixing a pacing problem at animatic: redraw two panels, adjust three timings,
re-export. Half a day.

Fixing the same pacing problem after animation: the two scenes either side need
their in and out points rebuilt, which means the transitions between them break,
which means the two scenes *those* connect to now feel wrong. The sound design is
cut to the old timings. Roughly a fortnight, and a nervous conversation about the
delivery date.

The animatic does not add a week to the schedule. It removes two from the back
end, where weeks are much more expensive because there is no slack left.

## Where scratch audio comes from

The usual objection: "the voiceover is not recorded yet."

It never is. Record it yourself on a phone. Have the producer read it. Use a
text-to-speech pass. It genuinely does not matter, because you are testing
duration and emphasis, not performance, and the real VO can be dropped in later
with only small timing adjustments.

What does matter is that the scratch is read at the pace the final will be read
at. Someone rushing through the script to get it done produces a scratch track
that is ten seconds shorter than reality, and you will discover the shortfall at
exactly the wrong moment.

## When you genuinely can skip it

There are two cases:

1. **You are remaking something.** A localisation of a piece that already exists,
   or a new version of last year's spot with new figures. The structure is proven.
2. **It is one continuous shot.** A single-take logo animation has no edit to
   test, so there is nothing an animatic would tell you that a style frame will
   not.

Everything else — and it really is everything else — benefits more from two days
of animatic than from two days of extra polish at the end.

> If the film does not work as a slideshow with someone reading over it, no amount
> of animation will make it work. It will just make it expensive.

## What we do

We quote the animatic as its own approved stage with its own sign-off, and we do
not start animation until it is signed. Not out of process rigidity — because the
alternative is a project where the structural conversation happens in week six
instead of week one, and by then it is not a conversation. It is a crisis.
