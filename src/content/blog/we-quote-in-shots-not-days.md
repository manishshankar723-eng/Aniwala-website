There are two ways to price creative production, and the difference between them
decides how the whole project feels.

**A day rate** says: here is what an artist costs per day, here is roughly how many
days we think it will take. It is easy to produce, easy to compare between
vendors, and it tells you almost nothing about your actual project.

**A shot count** says: we watched your cut or read your asset list, here is every
item, sorted by complexity, with a number attached to each. It takes us longer to
produce. It is the only one of the two that survives contact with reality.

## Why the difference matters

Halfway through every project, something changes. A sequence gets recut. A
character is cut and two are added. The publisher wants the level in snow as well
as rain.

With a day rate, you now have a negotiation. How many extra days? Who decides?
Was that in the original estimate? Nobody can answer from the document, so the
answer comes from whoever is more stubborn.

With a shot count, you have arithmetic. Six shots came out, four went in, three of
the new ones are band B and one is band C. The new number is not a matter of
opinion — it is the same rates applied to a different list, and it takes ten
minutes rather than a week and a difficult call.

That is the entire argument. A shot count converts the most stressful conversation
in production into a calculation.

## What a bid actually looks like

For a VFX or animation job we send a spreadsheet with a row per shot:

- Shot ID and duration
- What the work is, in one line
- A complexity band — A, B or C, defined explicitly at the top of the sheet
- Assumptions specific to that shot ("assumes clean plate supplied")
- Days, and therefore cost

For game art, the same structure with assets instead of shots: asset name, band,
polygon and texture budget, whether concept is included.

The assumptions column is the important one and the one most bids leave out. Half
of all overruns trace back to something the vendor quietly assumed and the client
quietly assumed differently. Writing them down does not prevent the disagreement —
it just moves it to week one, where it is free.

## The bands are the honest part

Band definitions are where a bid is either useful or decorative. Ours read like
this, adjusted per project:

- **Band A** — the work is well understood and similar to something on the list
  already. Low risk. If we are wrong, we are wrong by hours.
- **Band B** — a known technique applied to a new problem. Some iteration
  expected. If we are wrong, we are wrong by a day.
- **Band C** — we have to solve something before we can build it. Genuinely
  uncertain. We will say what would reduce the uncertainty, and often it is a
  half-day test we would rather do before the project starts than during it.

If everything in a bid is band A, the vendor has not read the brief carefully.
Real projects always have a few Cs, and a bid that hides them is not cheaper — it
is just wrong later.

## What we do about being wrong

We will be wrong on some shots. Everyone is. The question is who absorbs it.

Our rule: **if we underestimated, that is ours.** A shot we banded A that turns
out to be B is our misjudgement, and we eat it. If the brief changed — new
reference, new note, new requirement that was not on the sheet — that is a change,
and we price it as one, before doing the work rather than after.

The distinction is not always perfectly clean, and when it is genuinely ambiguous
we would rather split it than argue about it. But having the rule written down
means the conversation starts from a shared principle instead of from whoever
emailed first.

> A quote is a prediction. The useful part is not the number — it is being able to
> see how the number was built, so you know what happens to it when something
> moves.

## What we need to build one

Enough to be specific: the cut with timecode, or the asset list; the delivery
spec; the date; and whatever reference exists for the look. If those do not exist
yet, we will give you a range with the assumptions stated, and replace it with a
real count as soon as they do.

What we will not do is send a single number with no working. It looks decisive,
and it is the least informative thing a studio can hand you.
